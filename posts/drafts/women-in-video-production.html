Where Are the Women in Video Production?

I've been in video production for 15 years. I can count on one hand the number of times I've walked into a production room and not been the only woman there.

This isn't a complaint post. It's an observation. And I think we need to talk about why this matters.

The Numbers Don't Lie

Video production—especially on the technical side—is still overwhelmingly male. Camera operators, audio engineers, technical directors, broadcast engineers. Walk into any control room or live production and look around. It's getting better. Slowly. But we're not there yet.

Why This Space Is Hard

Let me be honest about what this job looks like:

  • Manual labor. We're hauling gear, running cables, building sets. It's physical work.
  • Long hours. Production days don't end at 5pm. Live events mean early call times and late wrap times. Sometimes both on the same day.
  • Unfriendly culture. I've worked with crews where the vibe is... not welcoming. Old-school production culture can be rough, dismissive, and sometimes hostile to anyone who doesn't fit the mold.

None of these things are inherently gendered. But they create an environment that has historically pushed women out or never let them in.

Why Representation Matters

Here's the thing: the people behind the camera shape what ends up on screen. When production teams lack diversity, the content reflects that. The stories we tell, the perspectives we capture, the voices we amplify—all of it is influenced by who's in the room making decisions.

Less representation behind the scenes means less representation on screen. And that's bad for everyone.

What's Changing

I am seeing more women entering the space, especially on the creative and post-production side. Editors, motion designers, producers. The pipeline is growing. But the technical production roles—the ones that require you to be on set, on location, in the trenches—still lag behind.

What Would Help

Some thoughts:

  • Mentorship. When I started, I didn't have women mentors in production. Finding people who look like you doing the thing you want to do matters.
  • Culture change. Production doesn't have to be a boys' club. Leadership sets the tone.
  • Visibility. The more we talk about women in these roles, the more normal it becomes. I want the next generation to not think twice about whether they belong in a control room.

My Experience

I've been lucky. I've built a team and a space where this isn't an issue. But I remember what it was like coming up. The assumptions. The proving yourself over and over. The exhaustion of being the only one.

We're making progress. But let's keep pushing.

To the women in video production: I see you. To the ones thinking about getting in: there's room for you here.
